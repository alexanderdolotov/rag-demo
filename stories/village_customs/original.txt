Every autumn the villagers of Maren Hollow keep the old customs of the harvest festival, just as their grandparents did. The celebration begins on the first Saturday after the last field has been cut. By midday the whole square is hung with braided straw and dried apples, and the long tables are carried out from the mill.

The oldest custom is the lantern walk. At dusk the children gather at the church gate with paper lanterns, and the eldest woman in the village lights each one from a single candle brought from her own hearth. They walk in silence to the orchard, where the lanterns are hung in the branches to thank the trees for the year's fruit.

Another custom is the sharing of the harvest loaf. The baker shapes it like a sheaf of wheat and carries it to the square on a wooden board. Nobody may cut it until the miller's youngest child has broken the first piece by hand, and every household must receive a piece, even strangers passing through. Visitors are told that refusing a piece brings bad luck for the winter.

The festival ends with dancing around a bonfire, and a final custom: each person throws a handful of grain into the flames and speaks a wish for the coming year. The older villagers say the wishes are only kept if they are never repeated aloud. By midnight the square is quiet again, the lanterns are glowing in the orchard, and woodsmoke lingers over the whole village.
