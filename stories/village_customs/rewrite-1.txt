Each fall the people of Maren Hollow carry on the age-old traditions of their harvest celebration, exactly as their grandparents did. It starts on the first Saturday after the final field is mown. By noon the square is draped with plaited straw and dried apples, and long tables are wheeled out of the mill.

The most ancient tradition is the lantern procession. At twilight the children meet at the church gate holding paper lanterns, and the village's oldest woman lights each one from a single candle carried from her own fireplace. They march quietly to the orchard and hang the lanterns on the boughs as thanks to the trees for the year's fruit.

Another tradition is sharing the festival bread, formed in the shape of a bundle of wheat. No one may slice it until the miller's youngest child has torn off the first piece with bare hands, and every family must be given a share, including travellers passing by. Guests are warned that turning down a piece invites misfortune all winter. The evening closes with dancing around a bonfire and one last tradition: everyone tosses a fistful of seed into the fire and whispers a wish. Elders insist a wish only comes true if it is never said out loud again.
