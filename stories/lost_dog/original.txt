The lost dog wandered through the neighborhood for most of a rainy Sunday. It was a medium-sized brown dog with a white patch on its chest and a frayed red collar, and it had slipped through a gap in the fence while its owner, Mr. Okafor, was carrying groceries from the car. By the time he noticed, the gate was swinging and the dog was gone.

The dog walked slowly along the wet streets, sniffing at lampposts and doorsteps, stopping to drink from a puddle. It passed the bakery, the closed school, the corner shop with its striped awning. Some people looked at it but nobody stopped, because they assumed it belonged to someone nearby. Twice it sat down under a bus shelter, tired and hungry, and looked up the road as if waiting for a familiar whistle.

Late in the afternoon a child named Mira, walking home from her piano lesson, saw the dog shivering behind a hedge. She read the tag on its collar, which had a phone number on it, and she crouched and spoke softly until it came to her. Mira walked it to her mother, who called the number. Within fifteen minutes Mr. Okafor arrived in his coat, and the dog threw itself at his legs. The child returned the dog to its owner with a proud, serious face.
