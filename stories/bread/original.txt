On Saturday morning Ana decided to bake bread. She had watched her grandfather do it a hundred times, and she remembered the order of things even if she had never done them herself. In her small kitchen she mixed flour, water, yeast and a pinch of salt in the big blue bowl, and when the mixture came together into a sticky mass she tipped it onto the floured table.

She kneaded the dough for ten minutes, pushing it away with the heel of her hand, folding it back, turning it a quarter. At first it clung to her fingers, but slowly it grew smooth and elastic, and when she pressed it with a fingertip the dent sprang back. She shaped it into a ball, set it in the bowl under a damp towel, and let it rise for an hour beside the warm stove.

When she lifted the towel the dough had doubled. She divided it in half, shaped two oval loaves, slashed the tops with a knife and slid them into the hot oven. The kitchen filled with the smell of baking. Forty minutes later she took out two golden loaves of bread, crusty and hollow-sounding when she tapped the bottoms. She let them cool on a rack, though she could not resist tearing off the end of one with butter.
