Saturday morning, Ana made up her mind to make a loaf. She had seen her grandfather do it countless times, and she remembered the steps even though she had never tried them herself. In her little kitchen she stirred flour, water, yeast and a little salt together in the big blue bowl, and once it formed a sticky lump she turned it out onto the floured counter.

She worked the mixture for ten minutes, shoving it away with the heel of her hand, folding it over and giving it a quarter turn. At first it stuck to her fingers, but gradually it became smooth and stretchy, and when she poked it the dent filled back in. She rolled it into a ball, covered it with a damp cloth in the bowl, and left it to proof for an hour near the warm stove.

When she took off the cloth it had doubled in size. She cut it in two, formed a pair of oval shapes, scored the tops and put them in the hot oven. The kitchen smelled wonderful. Forty minutes later out came a pair of brown loaves with a crisp crust that sounded hollow when she knocked on the bottoms. She set them on a rack to cool, but could not help tearing the end off one and eating it with butter.
