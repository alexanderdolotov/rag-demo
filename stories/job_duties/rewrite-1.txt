Walter Pruitt had been the night guard at the Aldous Museum for twenty-two years, and he could list his responsibilities in his sleep. At ten o'clock he locked the main doors and the side entrance. At eleven he started the first of his rounds, walking through every gallery on three floors with a heavy torch and checking that each window was fastened. On the hour he wrote down the time and the word 'clear' in the log book, as the rules said he must.

The rest of his job was less obvious. He read the thermometer and humidity dial in the painting rooms, since the old canvases cracked if the air became too dry. He wound the big clock in the entrance hall every Thursday, and at two in the morning he went down to the cellar to look at the boiler. He was also supposed to water the ferns in the atrium, a task no rule listed but which the director quietly appreciated.

One March night, on his three o'clock round, Walter felt a cold draught in the east gallery. A small window above the staircase stood open. Nothing was missing and nobody was there, but he shut it, wrote the time and place in the log and phoned the director at home. A watchman's work, he liked to say, is mostly looking, and noticing what is different from yesterday.
