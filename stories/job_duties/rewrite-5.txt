Nothing was stolen from the Aldous Museum one March night, thanks to the habits of its night guard, Walter Pruitt, who noticed a cold draught on his three o'clock round and found a small stairway window open.

Pruitt, who has held the post for twenty-two years, shut the window, wrote the time and place in his log and telephoned the director at home. It was the kind of detail his job depends on. Each night he locks the main and side doors at ten, patrols all three floors from eleven with a heavy torch, checks that windows are latched and writes the time and the word clear in the book every hour.

He also checks the temperature and humidity dials in the painting rooms, winds the hall clock on Thursdays, looks at the basement boiler at two a.m. and waters the atrium ferns, a task nobody assigned. The work, he says, is mostly looking and noticing what has changed since yesterday.
