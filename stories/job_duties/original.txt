Walter Pruitt had been the night watchman at the Aldous Museum for twenty-two years, and he could recite his duties in his sleep. At ten o'clock he locked the main doors and the side entrance. At eleven he began the first of his rounds, walking every gallery on three floors with a heavy flashlight and checking that each window was latched. On the hour he wrote the time and the word 'clear' in the log book, as the rules required.

His other duties were less obvious. He checked the thermometer and humidity dial in the painting rooms, because the old canvases cracked if the air got too dry. He wound the big clock in the entrance hall every Thursday, and he went down to the basement at two in the morning to look at the boiler. He was also expected to water the ferns in the atrium, which no rule mentioned but which the director quietly appreciated.

On a night in March, during his three o'clock round, Walter noticed a cold draught in the east gallery. A small window above the staircase was open. Nothing was missing and no one was there, but he closed it, noted the time and the location in the log, and telephoned the director at home. The duties of a watchman, he liked to say, are mostly looking, and noticing what is different from yesterday.
