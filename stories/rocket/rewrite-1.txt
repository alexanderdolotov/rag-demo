The launch vehicle left the pad at midday under a clear, breezy sky. Crowds on the beach a few kilometres off watched the white machine climb on a pillar of flame, slowly at first and then more quickly, until the noise arrived as a long rolling rumble. Mission control called out each step: engine start, passage through the point of greatest air pressure, and the cutoff of the first stage.

It arrived in space in under ten minutes. The upper stage burned smoothly, the nose cover split and dropped into the sea, and the vehicle slipped into a round path 800 kilometres above the planet. In the control room the engineers cheered and embraced after months of testing and two postponements for bad weather.

On board was a climate observation craft, the first of a new family built to follow storms and measure sea temperatures. Once released it opened its solar panels and radioed the ground within twenty minutes. The agency said it would start returning data in roughly six weeks, after its instruments are checked, and forecasters expect it to give hurricane warnings several hours more notice.
