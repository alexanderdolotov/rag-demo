The rocket lifted off from the coastal launch site at noon on a clear, windy day. Spectators lined the beach a few kilometres away and watched the white vehicle rise on a column of fire, slowly at first and then faster, until the sound reached them as a long, rolling crackle. Mission control announced each milestone: engine ignition, the pass through maximum aerodynamic pressure, then the shutdown of the first stage.

The rocket reached orbit nine minutes later. The second stage burned steadily, the payload fairing split away and fell into the ocean, and the vehicle settled into a circular orbit 800 kilometres above the Earth. In the control room engineers applauded and hugged each other after months of testing and two delays caused by weather.

It was carrying a weather satellite, the first of a new series designed to track storms and measure ocean temperatures. After the stage separation the satellite unfolded its solar panels and sent its first signal to the ground station within twenty minutes. The agency said the satellite would begin sending forecast data in about six weeks, once its instruments had been calibrated. Meteorologists said the new data would improve hurricane warnings by several hours.
