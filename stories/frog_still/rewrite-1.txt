The toad refused to hop all day. It had woken in its usual spot among the cattails at the shallow side of the marsh, expecting an ordinary morning: a few quiet calls, a hop onto the flat rock to dry off in the sun, then a long paddle to the floating leaves where the insects collected.

Then it noticed the large bird. A tall gray wader was standing in the water only a short distance away, frozen in place, one leg tucked up and its long neck bent in a curve. The bird's yellow eye was trained on the water. The toad knew that even a small twitch would be fatal.

So it froze too. It pressed its belly into the cool mud and let its brownish-green back melt into the cattails. The hours crawled by. The sun rose, the fog lifted, and the heat settled over the marsh. Insects crawled across its face and it ignored them. Once the big bird stepped slowly closer, and the toad's pulse thundered, but not a muscle moved.

Late in the day the bird struck, pulled out a tiny silver fish, gulped it down and resumed its watch. Not until the sky went orange and then dim, and the bird finally spread its heavy wings and flew off over the trees, did the toad breathe out. It remained frozen among the cattails until night had fully fallen, and only then did it creep away.
